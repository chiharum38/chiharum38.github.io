Background

Starting one level lower than usual

Most battery studies begin by downloading a price series. That is a reasonable place to start if you want a business case, but it takes the most interesting object in the problem — the price itself — entirely on trust.

Electricity prices are not an input to a power system. They are the dual variables of a constrained optimisation: the marginal cost of serving one more MW of demand, which falls out of the dispatch problem rather than being assigned to it. That is the theoretical content of nodal pricing, stated in Schweppe’s Spot Pricing of Electricity and in Kirschen and Strbac.

This project demonstrates that from published cost data rather than asserting it, and then uses the resulting prices to work out what a battery is actually worth.

The five steps: solve economic dispatch on the IEEE 118-bus quadratic cost curves by lambda iteration → verify numerically that the multiplier equals the derivative of system cost with respect to demand → build an hourly price series by pushing a demand profile through that dispatch → optimise battery arbitrage against those prices as a linear program → report sensitivities, because the demand profile is an assumption.
Results, part one

The duality result, verified

Lambda iteration finds the system marginal price by adjusting a single scalar until total dispatched generation matches demand. At the optimum, every dispatched unit should be operating where its own marginal cost equals that scalar. And the scalar itself should equal the derivative of total system cost with respect to demand — which can be checked numerically by nudging demand and re-solving.

The multiplier and the numerical derivative agree to four decimal places, and the maximum deviation between any dispatched unit’s marginal cost and lambda is exactly zero. That is the theory of marginal pricing demonstrated on real published cost data rather than taken on faith.

The resulting price series

Pushing a daily demand profile through the dispatch produces 2,160 hours of prices: mean 37.51 EUR/MWh, range 30.36 to 41.00, standard deviation 3.07.

Finding one

Revenue per MWh of installed energy falls as duration rises. A one-hour battery earns 265 EUR per MWh installed; an eight-hour battery earns 104 — less than half.

The reason is that a short battery captures the single best spread each day and cycles fully, while a longer battery has to reach into shallower parts of the price curve to fill itself. Cycle count tells the same story: 83 cycles at one hour, 47 at eight hours. That points the opposite way from the intuition that bigger is better, and it is the kind of result a developer would act on when sizing an asset.

Does forecasting matter?

The LP above assumes perfect foresight of tomorrow’s prices, which no operator has. Re-running it with naive persistence — bid today assuming today’s prices repeat yesterday’s — gives the size of that advantage.

CaseRevenue over 89 days
Perfect foresight24,121 EUR
Naive persistence, yesterday’s prices22,508 EUR
Foresight premium6.7%

A 6.7 percent foresight premium is smaller than I expected, and I want to be clear that this is a property of my assumed profile rather than a fact about batteries. My daily demand shape repeats closely, so yesterday is an excellent forecast of today. In a real market with weather-driven renewables the day-to-day shape varies far more, and the premium would be substantially larger. The result is reported because it is honest, not because it generalises.

Finding two, the most useful in the project

Below an amplitude of about 0.75, revenue is exactly zero. Not small — zero.

With a round-trip efficiency of 0.846, the battery only earns anything if the ratio between its selling price and its buying price exceeds 1.181. Below that threshold, every possible trade loses more to conversion losses than it gains on the spread, and the optimiser correctly refuses to trade at all.

So a battery business case is not proportional to average price, and it is not even proportional to price standard deviation. It depends on whether the achievable spread clears a hard efficiency barrier — and under that barrier the asset earns nothing from arbitrage regardless of how large it is. The observed price ratio in this study is 1.351, comfortably above the 1.181 break-even, which is why revenue exists at all.

This is the finding with the clearest commercial reading. An investment memo that models battery revenue as a linear function of price volatility will overstate it badly in a low-spread market, because the true function is zero up to a threshold and then rises steeply. The shape matters more than the slope.

Honesty

Limitations

The daily demand profile is assumed, not measured. It is the weakest input in the model, and every conclusion that leans on it is reported as a sensitivity rather than a point estimate. That is why the volatility scan exists.
  • The IEEE 118-bus system is heavily over-capacitated — 9,161 MW of generation against 4,242 MW of demand — so prices move far less than in a real tight market and no scarcity pricing is possible. Real markets spend part of the year on the steep end of the merit order, which this one never reaches.
  • Single bus, so no network constraints and no locational price differences. Congestion is a large part of why real nodal prices differ, and none of it appears here.
  • Day-ahead arbitrage only. No balancing, frequency response or capacity revenue. In the Nordic system, reserve revenue often exceeds arbitrage revenue — so this understates what a real battery earns, possibly by a lot.
  • No degradation cost, so the model cycles more freely than an operator would. Adding a per-cycle cost would push optimal duration up, not down.
  • Price-taker assumption, reasonable for 50 MW in a 4 GW system but not for a large fleet.
