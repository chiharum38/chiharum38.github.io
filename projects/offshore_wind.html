Background

Why a long AC cable fights you

An overhead line and a submarine cable are not the same component wearing different clothes. A cable is two conductors separated by a thin layer of insulation over its whole length, which makes it a very large distributed capacitor. Energise it and it draws charging current whether or not anybody is using the power at the far end.

That charging current has two consequences, and both get worse with length. It occupies conductor capacity that would otherwise carry useful power, and it pushes the voltage up at the lightly loaded end — the Ferranti effect. Shunt reactors absorb the excess reactive power and pull the voltage back down. The engineering question is how much reactor you need, and whether there is a length past which no amount of it is enough.

This project answers that for one specific design: a 200 MW offshore wind farm connected to shore at 110 kV.

Why this matters commercially: the HVAC-versus-HVDC decision is one of the largest single cost drivers in an offshore wind project. HVDC converter stations are expensive and are only justified once AC stops working. Knowing where that crossover sits, for a given voltage and rating, is what the study is for.
Network data

The cable

The model uses a pandapower standard line type rather than invented parameters, so the numbers trace back to a published source. The type is N2XS(FL)2Y 1x300 RM/35 64/110 kV, a single-core XLPE cable with a 300 mm² conductor, whose parameters come from Heuck, Dettmann and Schulz (2013), p. 744.

Methodology

What the code does

Step 1, a two-bus model

An offshore bus carrying the 200 MW generator, an onshore slack bus, and two parallel cable circuits between them. Deliberately the smallest model that contains the physics in question: nothing here depends on network topology, so a larger network would add noise rather than realism.

Step 2, sweep the length

Cable length runs from 10 km to 200 km in 10 km steps. At each length the model solves AC power flow uncompensated and records the offshore voltage, the circuit loading, the losses and the charging power.

Step 3, find the minimum compensation by bisection

At each length the code searches for the smallest shunt compensation ratio — the fraction of the cable’s charging power that gets cancelled — which keeps the offshore voltage inside 0.94 to 1.06 per unit and both circuits below their thermal rating. Bisection is used rather than a linear scan because the feasibility boundary is monotonic in compensation, so halving the interval converges in a few iterations instead of dozens of power flows.

Compensation is split evenly between the offshore and onshore ends.

Results

How much compensation, and where AC stops

Charging power scales linearly with length, as the physics demands: 54.7 Mvar at 50 km, 109.5 Mvar at 100 km, 219.0 Mvar at 200 km. At 100 km the cable is generating reactive power equal to 55 percent of the entire farm’s active power rating, purely by being switched on.

The requirement is not linear, and that is the interesting part. Out to 80 km the connection needs no compensation at all — the circuits are lightly enough loaded to absorb the charging current and the voltage stays in band on its own. From 90 km the requirement appears and then climbs steeply, reaching 98.8 percent at 160 km.

Key finding

160 km is the wall. Beyond it the offshore voltage leaves the band even with the charging power completely cancelled. Full compensation removes the shunt capacitance from the problem, and what remains is series impedance — the voltage drop and rise caused by current flowing through resistance and reactance, which no shunt reactor can fix.

That is the point where HVDC stops being an alternative to HVAC and becomes the only option.

The second finding: compensation buys thermal capacity too

Shunt reactors are usually discussed as a voltage control measure. They are also a loss and loading measure, because removing reactive current from the conductor leaves more room for real power.

Honesty

Limitations

The 160 km figure is specific to this design and should not be quoted as a general AC-versus-DC crossover distance. Published work often puts the practical HVAC limit far shorter. Zhu, Dui and Zhang (2015) state in their abstract that HVAC is preferred within 40 km of shore. This study reaches further because the two circuits are lightly loaded and therefore have spare capacity for charging current.
  • Only 110 kV is studied. Real export systems often use 220 kV or higher, which roughly halves the current for the same power and extends the AC range substantially.
  • The farm operates at unity power factor. Modern turbine converters can provide reactive support, which would change the compensation requirement.
  • Compensation is split evenly between the two ends, which Zhu et al. (2015) show is not optimal.
  • No mid-cable compensation platform, which is the standard industry method for extending AC reach and is exactly what Dakic et al. (2021) study.
  • No cost modelling. The real technology choice is economic, not just electrical. This study finds where AC becomes impossible, not where it becomes uneconomic — and the second boundary always comes first.

Tools and libraries

Software used

  • pandapower 2.14.1, AC power flow and the standard line type library that supplies the cable parameters.
  • numpy 1.26.4, the bisection search and the sweep arithmetic.
  • pandas 2.1.4, results tables and CSV export.
